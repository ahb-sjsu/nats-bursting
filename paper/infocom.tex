\title{Polite Bursting: What Feedback Is Worth When Admissions
Land Late on a Shared Cluster}

\ifblind\else
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Andrew H. Bond}
\IEEEauthorblockA{San Jose State University\\andrew.bond@sjsu.edu}}
\fi

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Research teams run batch GPU work on shared clusters they do not own. Fair-use
policy requires each pod a guest holds to stay busy and scrutinizes guests that
hold many at once, so a guest has to restrain itself. How much capacity a guest
can actually use is set by the other tenants. It is not announced, and a guest
learns it only by probing. Worse, a decision acts on information from before the
probe and takes effect only after a new worker starts, so the state it is judged
against has moved on by the time it lands.
We show that acting on that lag has an exact ceiling. For a contended resource
that is either free or taken, the extra goodput any feedback controller can win
over the best fixed budget, compared at equal over-admission, equals half the
correlation between capacity when the decision lands and capacity as it was last
seen. A rule that admits work whenever the last reading showed the resource free
attains that ceiling. The ceiling falls toward zero once the lag grows past the
time over which neighbour load stays correlated, so past that point feedback is
worth little however it is built. We give the elementary derivation rather than
claim it, since the ingredients are classical.
We turn the result into one controller. It estimates the correlation from its own
probe stream and switches between a feedback loop and a fixed budget, and we
bound how fast any rule can learn which of the two to run. That learning cost
grows with the square root of the lag even though the ceiling it chases decays
exponentially in the same quantity, and the hardest predictability level to sit at
is an intermediate one rather than either extreme.
Short controlled experiments measure the lag at about 2.5 seconds on a two-GPU
node, dominated by worker start-up, and exercise the controller against a
competing workload we drive ourselves. Under structured neighbour load feedback
admission delivers 1.41 times the goodput of a fixed trickle at a quarter of a
greedy policy's over-admission while leaving the competitor more of its own
throughput. Under memoryless load it only breaks even, which the ceiling predicts,
and the switch detects that case and stops paying for the loop. The live
comparison is at one operating point and so measures the system rather than the
constant.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Much machine-learning work is bursty. Long stretches of local development are
punctuated by short runs of batch jobs, sweeps, fine-tunes and evaluations, that
briefly need far more GPUs than a team owns. Shared clusters such as the National
Research Platform \cite{nautilus} make that capacity available on guest terms.
Fair-use policy caps concurrent pods per user, requires sustained utilization of
what is requested, forbids idle or sleep-only work, and reclaims resources on
completion. The guest also sits outside the cluster network, often reachable only
through one forwarded port or a mesh VPN, with no inbound access.

Two obvious strategies both fail here. Submitting everything at once takes more
than a fair share, leaves pods pending or idle, and trips the thresholds that get
a namespace banned. A fixed submission rate is safe but wastes the guest's own
throughput whenever capacity is free. Neither one reacts to what the cluster is
doing.

Being a good guest is a control problem, and how well it can be solved depends on
how stale the guest's view of the cluster is. This paper makes that dependence
exact and then builds admission algorithms on it.

Our first result is a ceiling on the value of feedback. When a controller acts on
a capacity reading of a given age, the extra goodput it can win over the best
fixed budget equals half the correlation between capacity now and capacity as
last seen. The ceiling is tight, since a rule that admits work whenever the last
reading showed capacity free reaches it. It also implies an impossibility. Once
the sensing delay exceeds the time over which neighbour load stays correlated,
the correlation is near zero and no feedback controller can beat a fixed budget.

Our second result turns the ceiling into a controller. The correlation it needs is
measurable from the probe stream it already collects, so a guest can estimate it
online and act on the estimate. Algorithm \ref{alg:adaptive} does this with a
handful of counters, switching between a feedback loop and a fixed budget, and on
average it trails the better of the two by an amount that shrinks with run
length. We bound the cost of that decision from both sides. We give matching upper and lower bounds on how fast
any rule can learn which branch to take, and together they show that the lag
costs only its square root in learning even though it costs exponentially in the
ceiling itself. The hardest predictability level to sit at is
neither high nor low but a specific value in between, which is exactly where a
controller holding no state has no good default. Integer capacity beyond one
contended unit is left open, for reasons \S\ref{sec:controller} states.

Our third contribution is measurement rather than proof. The sensing delay is a
physical quantity and we measure it at roughly 2.5 seconds on real hardware,
dominated by CUDA start-up rather than by polling. We then drive a two-GPU node with a
competing workload of our own construction and show the predicted behaviour on
both sides of the crossover, including a case where feedback stops paying for itself and the
switching algorithm gives it up.

The control plane runs inside the cluster and reaches the home node over a single
outbound port. The fabric that carries it is characterized in a companion
paper~\cite{companion} and is treated here as given, since what matters for the
result is only that the guest's view of capacity arrives late.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:bg}
\textbf{Host policy.} The reference host \cite{nautilus} publishes a fair-use
policy with two features our design depends on. Utilization relative to request
must stay within published bands for each running pod, and holding many
concurrent pods brings a namespace under closer scrutiny for exactly that
condition. Jobs must do real work, since sleep-only jobs are banned, and must
release resources on completion. Administrators monitor utilization and act on
namespaces that persistently hold under-utilized requests, so restraint is not
optional.

We therefore do \emph{not} model the policy as an absolute pod maximum. The
published rules attach utilization conditions to concurrency rather than
forbidding it outright, and the permitted concurrency depends on resource type
and node. Instead $K$ is a \emph{self-imposed politeness window} that the guest
sets at or below the concurrency at which those conditions begin to apply, and
$\epsilon$ is the share of under-utilized epochs the guest is willing to risk.
This is the weaker and more defensible reading, and nothing below depends on the
particular value. The experiments use $K{=}4$ and a contended $C{=}2$ node.

\textbf{Network reality.} The guest reaches the cluster over one forwarded port
or a mesh VPN, with no inbound access. A control plane must therefore live behind
that boundary and communicate over a fabric that traverses it, which motivates
the leaf-node design of \S\ref{sec:fabric}.

\textbf{Why existing tools do not settle it.} Workflow and function-execution
frameworks such as HTCondor \cite{htcondor}, funcX and Globus Compute
\cite{funcx} and Ray \cite{ray} scale workers to demand and carry no notion of a
host politeness budget. Left to
default scaling they over-provision under contention. We make self-restraint a
controlled objective instead.

\section{Realization: Sensing over a Federated Fabric}
\label{sec:fabric}
The controller runs inside the cluster, behind the network boundary, and reaches
the home node over a NATS \cite{nats} leaf link that needs one outbound port
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). It probes cluster occupancy and actuates admission across
that link. Submissions flow on \texttt{burst.submit} and results return on
\texttt{results.>} by interest propagation, with no inbound cluster access. The capacity feedback itself is local, since the controller and the workers both
sit inside the cluster, so the leaf link carries submissions and results rather
than the capacity signal. What matters for the controller is that its view of
capacity is late for the reasons of \eqref{eq:Ddef}. The leaf round trip and the
CUDA start-up of a freshly admitted pod together set the information-to-effect lag
of \S\ref{sec:limit}, which we measure in \S\ref{sec:eval}. Tensors crossing the
link are compressed by random-rotation quantization
\cite{turboquantpro,turboquant}, which helps bandwidth on the hop but does not
reduce that lag, since it is dominated by CUDA start-up rather than transport. The fabric's own design, its leaf federation, durability and
single-port traversal, is characterized in the companion paper~\cite{companion}.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
\sysname{} is a Go control plane with a decider, prober, submitter, NATS bridge
and back-off modules, plus a Python client and an IPython cell magic
\cite{natsbursting}. The controller runs inside the cluster under a minimal
namespace-scoped ServiceAccount holding Job create, get and delete rights and pod
read, connects to the in-cluster NATS leaf, and renders each
\texttt{JobDescriptor} into a Kubernetes Job. Node targeting through a
\texttt{nodeSelector} on the descriptor pins guest pods to non-reserved GPU
types. We verified the full path live on the reference host \cite{nautilus}. A
submit from the home node propagated through hub, leaf and controller, launched a
GPU worker that ran real CUDA work, and published its result back through the
leaf to the home driver. The system, the controller and worker images, and the
transport and right-sizing packages \cite{turboquantpro,batchprobe} are
\ifblind available as open-source artifacts, with repositories anonymized for
review\else open source\fi.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Bursting and function execution.} HTCondor \cite{htcondor}, funcX and
Globus Compute \cite{funcx} and Ray \cite{ray} provide elastic task execution and
provider-driven scaling, and treat the host as owned capacity to fill. None of them models a fair-use politeness
budget or admission under a delayed view of capacity. They are complementary
substrates, and a head-to-head comparison against them appears in the companion
systems paper~\cite{companion}.

\textbf{Congestion and scavenger control.} Our loop is AIMD in the lineage of
additive-increase and multiplicative-decrease congestion avoidance
\cite{chiujain}, and its objective of yielding to incumbents and using only slack
aligns with low-priority transports such as LEDBAT \cite{ledbat} and TCP Nice
\cite{tcpnice}. Those protocols back off on one-sided loss or delay signals. Two
things differ here. The objective is two-sided, since the guest must stay under a
pod cap and above a utilization floor, which admits an over-admission measure
rather than pure loss. And we bound what any such rule can achieve rather than
proposing one more back-off schedule.

\textbf{Opportunistic and harvesting schedulers.} Running low-priority work in
another tenant's slack is well established. AntMan co-schedules opportunistic
deep-learning jobs against production ones on shared GPUs~\cite{antman}, Harvest
VMs sell reclaimable idle cloud capacity with an SLO for the
primary~\cite{harvestvm}, and Gavel~\cite{gavel} and Pollux~\cite{pollux} adapt
GPU allocations dynamically. All of them are operated by the party that owns the
capacity, so available capacity is observable and the constraint is one-sided,
namely do not harm the primary. The guest here owns nothing, cannot see the
neighbour directly, and must also stay \emph{above} a floor on what it does hold.

\textbf{Learned resource control.} Deep reinforcement learning schedulers
including DeepRM \cite{deeprm} and Decima \cite{decima}, with variance reduction
for exogenous input processes \cite{inputdriven}, learn scheduling policies from
cluster feedback. Our ceiling applies to them unchanged, since it constrains any
causal policy rather than any particular family, and \S\ref{sec:eval-law} tests a
policy-gradient agent of that family against it.

\textbf{Age of information.} The ceiling is a statement about freshness, since the
value of feedback is set by the age of the capacity estimate relative to how fast
the neighbour changes. That places polite bursting in the age-of-information line
of work \cite{aoi}, on the control side rather than the sampling side. We bound
the achievable goodput and politeness frontier as a function of that lag and give a
controller that adapts to the measured value.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
Restraint on a shared cluster is a control problem whose difficulty is fixed by
how far its decisions land behind the state they are judged against. We gave that
dependence an exact form. The extra goodput a feedback controller can win over the
best fixed budget, compared at equal over-admission, is half the correlation
between capacity when the decision lands and capacity as last seen. It is attained
by admitting work whenever the last reading showed capacity free, and it falls
toward zero once that lag outgrows the neighbour's correlation time.

One controller follows from that form. It measures the correlation online and
switches between a feedback loop and a fixed budget, trailing the better of the two
on average by an amount that shrinks with run length, and it never exceeds the
guest's own window, which holds deterministically rather than in expectation. We
also bound from both sides how hard
it is to decide which branch to run, and that cost grows only with the square root
of the lag even though the ceiling it chases decays exponentially in the same
quantity. Converting that decision cost into a goodput guarantee needs the value of
each branch under a Markov neighbour, which we leave open.

Short controlled experiments support the result on both sides of the crossover.
Under structured neighbour load, feedback admission reaches 1.41 times a fixed
budget's goodput at a quarter of a greedy policy's over-admission while leaving
the competing tenant more throughput. Under memoryless load it breaks even, and
the switching algorithm detects the case and stops paying for a loop that cannot
help. A learned policy-gradient controller given a longer observation history converges
to the ceiling and does not pass it, on the process the theorem assumes.

Four things remain open. Contention is manufactured on a two-GPU node rather than
drawn from a production multi-tenant trace, and the live comparison is at one
operating point rather than a matched-over-admission frontier, so it measures the
system rather than the constant. The exact equality holds for a binary Markov
unit, while the general statement is the scalarized bound of
App.~\ref{app:beta}. Integer capacity needs the nesting and monotonicity
conditions of \S\ref{sec:controller} before it can be claimed. And a controller
that estimates the correlation itself, rather than only which side of the switch it
falls on, should recover part of the distance between AIMD and the ceiling.
