\begin{abstract}
Research teams often burst batch GPU work onto shared clusters they do not own.
A guest can restrain its own concurrency, but the capacity left by other users is
not announced and a capacity record may be stale by the time a newly admitted
worker becomes productive. We study one exogenous binary unit and compare
feedback with the best record-independent policy at the same capacity-mismatch
rate. For symmetric Markov availability, the exact maximum goodput increase is
one half of the correlation between the state at observation and at action
effect. A delayed threshold rule attains the bound, while the increase vanishes
as the observation-to-effect lag exceeds the capacity coherence time. We then
give a branch-adaptive controller that estimates the transition rate and chooses
between a feedback branch and a static branch. A local decision-theoretic result
gives its branch-selection rate without claiming that AIMD itself attains the
optimal frontier. Controlled experiments measure a launch-on-demand effect lag
of about 2.5 seconds. On a two-GPU node with a controlled competing workload,
the feedback branch obtains 1.41 times the goodput of one static operating point
under structured contention, at a different mismatch rate, while the two break
even under memoryless contention. A matched-constraint Markov sweep verifies the
analytical frontier and a separate live sweep checks the estimator and shows the
mismatch reduction obtained by disabling feedback as predictability falls.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Machine-learning work is often bursty. Long periods of development are followed
by short runs of sweeps, fine-tunes and evaluations that need more accelerators
than a team owns. Shared clusters such as the National Research Platform
\cite{nautilus} make that capacity available under usage rules that discourage
persistently under-utilized requests \cite{nrppolicy}. A guest can suppress its
own submissions before they reach the scheduler, but it does not control
placement and does not observe the capacity left by other users directly.

This paper asks a narrow question. How much useful admission can a guest gain
from delayed capacity feedback if it is compared with the best static policy at
the same rate of offering more concurrency than can be productive at that time.
The mismatch rate is a guest-side risk proxy. It is not itself a claim that every
mismatch is a host-policy violation, since a Kubernetes excess offer may remain
pending rather than occupy a resource.

Our first result gives an exact answer for one symmetric binary Markov unit. The
maximum increase over a record-independent policy equals one half of the
correlation between the observed state and the state when the admission takes
effect. The statement is a converse over every causal controller in the stated
model and uses a matched mismatch constraint. A delayed threshold policy reaches
the bound. As the observation-to-effect lag grows beyond the coherence time, the
possible increase becomes small.

Our second result turns the predictability calculation into one adaptive
controller. The controller estimates the Markov flip probability from the
capacity samples it already receives and selects either an AIMD feedback branch
or a static branch. We keep two claims separate. The exact frontier is achieved
by the threshold policy, not by AIMD. The learning result concerns only how fast
a selector can identify the better of its two supplied branches under an
explicit local crossing assumption. It does not remove any structural shortfall
inside those branches. Integer capacity beyond one contended unit remains open.

Our third contribution is controlled measurement. We distinguish the age of a
capacity record at decision time from the additional delay until a launch-on-
demand worker becomes productive. Their sum is the observation-to-effect lag in
the theory. The local occupancy poll takes tens of milliseconds, while the
measured launch path takes about 2.5 seconds. We then run a controlled competing
workload on a two-GPU node. The live experiments demonstrate one useful operating
point, a pre-registered break-even case, and the mechanics of the online switch.
A separate discrete-time Markov sweep performs the matched-constraint test of the
closed form.

The controller runs inside the cluster and the home client communicates over an
outbound NATS leaf connection. The capacity probe itself is local to the cluster.
NATS is therefore the submission and result path in this implementation, not the
source of the measured capacity-record delay.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:bg}
\textbf{Host policy.}
The reference host publishes utilization conditions for users submitting more
than about four violating pods and asks users to match requests to expected use
\cite{nrppolicy}. These rules are not an absolute four-pod cluster cap. We use
$K$ as a self-imposed concurrency window chosen by the guest. The mismatch budget
$\epsilon$ is also guest chosen and is not inferred from the host policy. This
separation avoids treating a model event as an administrative violation.

\textbf{Network boundary.}
The home client reaches the in-cluster control plane over one outbound connection,
with no inbound cluster access. NATS request and result delivery is useful under
that boundary. The capacity record and scheduler outcome used by the controller
are produced inside the cluster.

\textbf{Why existing tools do not settle the question.}
Workflow and function-execution frameworks such as HTCondor \cite{htcondor},
funcX and Globus Compute \cite{funcx}, and Ray \cite{ray} scale workers to demand.
They do not define a guest-side mismatch budget under delayed exogenous capacity.
Our focus is the achievable admission frontier under that information limit.

\section{Realization Across a Federated Boundary}
\label{sec:fabric}
The controller runs inside the cluster and reaches the home client over a NATS
\cite{nats} leaf connection that needs one outbound port
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Submissions flow on \texttt{burst.submit} and results
return on \texttt{results.>} by interest propagation. The capacity probe is local
to the controller and workers, so leaf transport is not included in the measured
observation-to-effect lag. The implementation records local sample time, decision
time, Job creation time and the time at which useful GPU work becomes visible.
This event separation is what connects the physical path to the lag in
\eqref{eq:Ddef}.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
\sysname{} is a Go control plane with a decider, prober, submitter, NATS bridge
and back-off module, plus a Python client. The controller uses a namespace-scoped
ServiceAccount with Job create, get and delete rights and Pod read access. It
renders each request into a Kubernetes Job and can constrain eligible nodes with
a \texttt{nodeSelector}. We verified the complete path on the reference host. A
submission from the home node propagated through hub, leaf and controller,
launched a GPU worker that executed CUDA work, and returned its result through
the leaf. The experiments below concern the admission logic and its local
capacity record. They do not characterize NATS performance or claim that the
network path creates the capacity lag.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Bursting and function execution.}
HTCondor \cite{htcondor}, funcX and Globus Compute \cite{funcx}, and Ray
\cite{ray} provide elastic task execution. They are complementary substrates.
The question here is how much a guest can gain from delayed exogenous capacity
information under a self-imposed mismatch budget.

\textbf{Congestion and scavenger control.}
The feedback branch uses AIMD in the lineage of congestion avoidance
\cite{chiujain}. Its goal of yielding to incumbents is related to LEDBAT
\cite{ledbat} and TCP Nice \cite{tcpnice}. Those protocols react to loss or delay.
Here the signal is a delayed capacity or mismatch record, and the theorem bounds
the value of any causal policy in the stated binary model rather than proposing a
new AIMD law.

\textbf{Opportunistic and harvesting schedulers.}
AntMan co-schedules opportunistic deep-learning jobs against production work
\cite{antman}. Harvest VMs expose reclaimable cloud capacity \cite{harvestvm},
and Gavel \cite{gavel} and Pollux \cite{pollux} adapt accelerator allocations.
Those systems are operated by the capacity owner. Our guest does not control the
scheduler and treats the residual capacity as exogenous.

\textbf{Delayed state and learned resource control.}
Old information in load balancing and admission has long been studied
\cite{mitzenmacher,goel,kim1985,brooks1972,katsikopoulos}. DeepRM
\cite{deeprm} and Decima \cite{decima} learn resource-control policies from
feedback. The exact converse here applies to any causal policy under the binary
Markov assumptions, including a learned one, because the newest delayed sample is
then sufficient. It does not apply unchanged to non-Markov processes in which a
longer history remains predictive.

\textbf{Freshness and value of information.}
Age of information measures the freshness of delivered updates \cite{aoi}. Our
lag additionally includes the time from decision to useful effect. The binary
frontier is a constrained value-of-information calculation. Appendix
\ref{app:beta} gives a separate scalarized bound for general stationary capacity
using absolute regularity.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
For one symmetric binary Markov unit, delayed capacity feedback has an exact
matched-constraint value. The maximum goodput increase over a record-independent
policy is one half of the correlation between the observed state and the state at
action effect. A delayed threshold policy attains the bound, and the increase
vanishes as the observation-to-effect lag outgrows the capacity coherence time.

We used that predictability measure in one branch-adaptive controller. Its online
estimator is simple because the symmetric-chain flip indicators are independent.
The local learning theorem concerns selection between a supplied feedback branch
and a supplied static branch. It does not claim that AIMD attains the analytical
frontier. The exact finite-$D$ sensitivity is retained in the theorem, with the
large-$D$ approximation stated only in its valid asymptotic domain.

The controlled system measures a launch-on-demand effect lag of about 2.5 seconds
and exercises the complete submission and result path. Under structured
contention, the feedback branch reaches 1.41 times the goodput of one static
operating point, but the comparison is not at matched mismatch. Under memoryless
contention the two break even. A discrete-time Markov sweep performs the matched
comparison and verifies the threshold frontier, while a separate two-seed live
sweep checks the estimator and the mismatch reduction of disabling feedback.

The main open work is empirical and structural. A live matched-mismatch frontier
needs more operating points and more repetitions. The completion-gated testbed
does not provide a fixed wall-clock lag. Warm workers or pipelining could reduce
the launch-on-demand effect delay at a resource cost and require a different
model. Random record ages, action-dependent capacity and nested multi-unit
admission also remain outside the exact result.
