These two are a well-matched pair, and the division of labor between them is unusually disciplined — the systems paper measures the fabric and explicitly refuses to re-prove the theory, the theory paper takes the fabric as given and explicitly refuses to re-characterize it, and each cites the other's scope boundary. The intellectual centerpiece — being a good guest is a control problem whose value is exactly ½·r(D), the autocorrelation of neighbour capacity at the sensing lag — is a genuinely elegant result: interpretable, falsifiable, and it converts your pre-registered null (Poisson contention, feedback breaks even) from an embarrassment into a *prediction*. That move, "the null is a theorem," is the best rhetorical asset either paper has. I checked the math and the arithmetic where I could, and most of it holds; the problems are in claim-placement and internal consistency, one of them serious. Paper by paper:

**The theory checks out.** I verified Theorem 1's LP by hand: the joint law from Lemma 1 gives g = [θ₁(1−q) + θ₀q]/2, ρ = [θ₁q + θ₀(1−q)]/2, the vertex at (θ₁,θ₀)=(1,0) is (q/2, (1−q)/2), static policies are pinned to the diagonal, and the vertical gap at the kink is (1−2q)/2 = ½r(D). ✓. Proposition 2's layer-cake also survives checking, including the subtle step: with the feedback policy m=x̂ᵘ and a static policy matched at the same *over-admission* (not the same marginal), the per-unit gain is Cov/(1−πᵤ) = πᵤrᵤ(D). ✓. Proposition 3's sawtooth fraction (D violating epochs per cycle of length (1−β)(a+Dα)/α) ✓, Theorem 3's Jensen-plus-TV argument and Corollary 3's β(D) = ½r(D) for the symmetric chain ✓. Even the systems paper's compression ratios reproduce exactly from the framing arithmetic (4096B → 520/392/264B with the 8-byte header gives 7.9/10.4/15.5×). One wording nit in Theorem 1: "at any matched over-admission level the maximum goodput gain … is ∆(D)" reads as though the gain equals ½r at *every* level; it's the maximum over levels, attained only at the kink ρ=q/2 (below the kink the gap is ρ(1−2q)/q < ½r). Small fix, but a theory reviewer will notice.

**The serious problem: the INFOCOM abstract claims its headline numbers were measured somewhere they weren't.** The abstract says the law is validated "on NRP Nautilus under real, manufactured contention: feedback admission is the polite-efficient point (up to 3.2× a static baseline's goodput while sparing the competing tenant…)". But per §VIII, Q2 and Q3 ran on a *controlled workstation testbed* (local-process workers; a two-GPU node) — deliberately, to escape scheduler variance that produced three nulls in situ — and only the end-to-end plumbing (§VII: one submit → one A10 worker → result) was verified on Nautilus. The conclusion repeats it: "end-to-end on NRP Nautilus, where under real GPU contention feedback admission is the polite-efficient knee — up to 3.2×." Worse, the 3.2× is stitched: 3.18× is the Q2 *abundance* CPU-pool result (self-imposed budget, no competing tenant), while "sparing the competing tenant" is the Q3 *square-contention* result, where the multiplier is 1.41×. The threats section tells the truth plainly — which makes the abstract's compression look like spin rather than error. Given that your entire research brand across xbse and the encoder paper is claims-at-licensed-strength, this is the one thing I'd insist on before submission: the abstract should say "on a workstation testbed with real GPU contention (full submission path verified end-to-end on NRP Nautilus): 1.41× static's goodput at 4× lower over-admission while sparing the neighbour under structured contention, and up to 3.2× under a self-imposed budget."

**The systems paper has three internal contradictions to reconcile.** First, the Background sentence "groups of pods must sustain utilization (5+ pods must each stay under 40% GPU)" states the NRP policy *backwards* — the companion paper and your own §VI ("94.4% … far above the >40% policy expectation") both make clear the floor is ≥40%; as written the sentence says the opposite of the constraint your whole design serves. Second, §VI.E's text says the pool scales "to 14.9k at eight [workers]" while Fig. 5's caption says "∼44k docs/s at eight workers, 5.4× one" — a 3× discrepancy for what reads as the same experiment (one is one GV100 in Table V's setup, the other two GV100s, and possibly different batch sizes, but the reader can't reconcile it and a reviewer will assume the worse explanation). Third, the politeness defaults (Table I: max concurrent = 50) sit oddly against the companion paper's statement that NRP policy caps K=4 concurrent pods per user — if the host cap is 4, a self-limit default of 50 is decorative; if 50 is right for some pod class, say which, and why the harness then restricted itself to ≤4.

**Smaller items, both papers.** The Table V comparison drives nats-bursting with the Go client and Ray/Dask/ProcessPool with Python clients — disclosed, but the bolded 0.9 ms dispatch win partially measures language runtime, not fabric; report the Python-client pool number alongside (you already have the harness, and the "different point in the design space" argument doesn't need the latency win anyway — the polite/WAN/bus columns are the real table). The cold-start n=5 is thin for a headline number; 20 runs is an afternoon. And on anonymity: the double-blind paper is internally clean, but it names the system "PoliteBurst," describes the `%%burst` magic, NRP Nautilus, and the leaf topology — one web search away from the PyPI packages and the SC paper, which openly cites the INFOCOM paper's exact title under your name. Venue norms usually tolerate discoverable preprints/artifacts, but check INFOCOM's policy text on companion disclosures, and consider whether reference [7] in the *public* systems paper should carry the anonymized title until reviews conclude.

Overall judgment: the theory paper is the stronger of the two — an exact, checkable law with a matching impossibility, an adaptive controller that provably tracks it, and a simulation that lands the optimal policy on the predicted curve to 0.013 is a complete story, and the honest Poisson null is its best figure. The systems paper is a solid practice-and-experience piece whose value is the politeness-as-mechanism design and the unusually honest measurement labeling (Little's-law plateau, warm-node cold-start caveats); its risk at review is "engineering glue," and its defense is exactly the lessons-learned section, which is genuinely useful to operators. Fix the abstract-evidence mismatch in one and the three contradictions in the other, and both are submittable. The pattern across your whole portfolio — pre-registered nulls treated as data, threats sections that outrank abstracts — is intact here in the bodies of both papers; the abstracts just need to be held to the same standard the sections already meet.
