These are a well-matched pair — a theory/control paper (INFOCOM-style, double-blind) and a practice/experience systems paper (SC26) that carve out genuinely disjoint contributions and cite each other carefully. The writing in both is confident and unusually honest (pre-registered nulls, Little's-law plateaus, "harness limits, not the bus"). Below is a review of each, followed by cross-cutting issues that only show up when reading them together.

## Paper 1: "Polite Bursting: Feedback Admission Control under Age-Limited Sensing"

**Summary and strengths.** The core result — the goodput advantage of any causal admission controller over the best static budget equals ½r(D), the capacity autocorrelation at the sensing lag — is clean, interpretable, and the kind of "one number explains both regimes" result reviewers like. The layer-cake lift (Prop. 2), the matching β-mixing converse in the appendix, and Corollary 3 showing the binary law is the tight instance of the general bound form a satisfying arc. The evaluation is well-designed: the optimal policy sits on the predicted curve within 0.013, the D/τc scaling collapse is convincing, and framing the live poisson null as a *prediction* of the theory rather than a failure is the paper's best rhetorical move. I verified the main calculations (the joint law in Lemma 1, the quadrilateral in Thm. 1, the sawtooth fraction in Prop. 3, the compression of β(D) to ½r(D) in Cor. 3) and they check out.

**Major concern 1 — the generality of Theorem 1 is overstated.** You define admissible policies as causal functions of the *entire* delayed history {x_{s−D}}_{s≤t}, but the proof of Thm. 1 parameterizes policies by (θ₁, θ₀) — functions of the single freshest sample x̂ₜ. For the symmetric Markov chain this is fine (x_{t−D} is a sufficient statistic for xₜ given the delayed history), but that sufficiency step is never stated and should be a one-line lemma. More importantly, the scope paragraph's claim that "the law (3) holds for any symmetric stationary xₜ via r(D)" is false for history-using controllers. Counterexample: a random-phase deterministic square wave with period 4D has r(D) = 0, yet the phase — and hence xₜ exactly — is recoverable from the delayed history, so a causal controller achieves ∆ = ½, not 0. The correct general statement is exactly what your appendix proves: ½r(D) is achievable in general (via the memoryless threshold), exact for Markov, and the converse is governed by β(D), not r(D). Corollary 1's "for any capacity process that is mixing (r(D)→0)" conflates the two; r(D)→0 does not imply unpredictability. Tightening this language costs nothing — the appendix already contains the right theorem — but as written, a careful reviewer will (correctly) call the abstract's "any causal admission controller" claim wrong outside the Markov case.

**Major concern 2 — the marquee controller is never run live.** The regime-adaptive controller (§V, Thm. 2) is the paper's practical payoff, but it's evaluated only in simulation on the exact two-state process the theory assumes — a somewhat circular validation — while the live contention experiments use plain AIMD. You acknowledge this in threats to validity, but given that the adaptive controller's entire pitch is "it detects and sidesteps the regime where AIMD fails," a single live poisson run showing it falling back to static would transform the paper. If it can't be done before the deadline, consider softening "never falls below static" in the abstract to make clear that's a simulation result.

**Minor issues.** (a) Notation collision: γ is the regime threshold in §V's box and the spectral gap in Thm. 2/Thm. 4 — rename one. (b) ρ is overloaded three ways: indicator fraction in (1), joint probability in (2), surplus expectation in Prop. 2; you note the C=2 coincidence, but a symbol distinction would help. (c) The abstract's "1.41× static's goodput at 4× lower over-admission" reads as 4× lower *than static*, but Table II shows aimd's ρ=0.15 vs static's 0.11 — the 4× is vs. naive. Rephrase to avoid the misreading. (d) The 2.5 s cold-start D and the 27 ms poll D are two different sensing ages depending on the question; the model uses a single fixed D, and the epoch-to-seconds mapping (D=3 epochs in Q4 vs D≈2.5 s in Q1) deserves one explicit sentence. (e) The politeness target ϵ is never calibrated against actual NRP policy — even a rough "the 40% floor corresponds to ϵ ≈ x under our epoch length" would tie the constraint to reality. (f) "Useful iff the guest can sense faster than the neighbour changes" — "iff" is doing more work than the math supports; the forward direction is a rate statement.

## Paper 2: "A Polite, Container-Native Bursting Fabric"

**Summary and strengths.** As a practice/experience paper this is strong: the numbers come from a continuously-running deployment (21-day leaf uptime), every measurement maps to a harness command, the artifact appendix is complete with DOIs, and the lessons-learned section (policy as first-class constraint, leaf federation leaking into the application, honest negatives) is exactly what this track wants. The measurement hygiene is notable — labeling the cold-start as warm-node/cached-image, explaining the warm-pool plateau via Little's law (64/0.076 ≈ 840/s checks out), excluding encode cost from the transport loop and saying so. The compression numbers are internally consistent (I re-derived 7.9/10.4/15.5× from the header + bit-packing arithmetic; the 2 Mbps hop timings match the payload sizes).

**Major concern — the politeness gate is asserted, not exercised.** The paper's title word is "polite," but Algorithm 1 is evaluated only through its indirect effects (utilization, warm-pool behavior); no experiment in this paper shows the gate actually deferring under load. You deliberately push the contention analysis to the companion, and the disjointness is well-managed — but a reviewer who doesn't have the companion will notice that the headline property is untested here. Even one small experiment — synthetic co-tenant pending queue crossing the courtesy threshold, gate flips to Backoff, exponential-with-jitter trace shown — would let this paper stand alone on its own claim without stealing the companion's thunder.

**Minor issues.** (a) Table V is apples-to-oranges in the dispatch column (Go client vs. Python clients); you acknowledge it and don't lean on the latency win, but bolding your own row while the footnote disclaims it invites a grumpy review — consider a table note marker on the 0.9 ms cell itself. (b) The 14.9k (Table V, one GV100, eight workers) vs. ~44k (Fig. 5, two GV100s) pairing is confusing on first read even with the parenthetical; give Fig. 5's config its own sentence. (c) "Parsl (HTEx) / funcX: by design" rows characterize systems without running them — defensible, but state the criterion for "by design" explicitly. (d) Cold-start n=20 mixing two node classes weakens the median; report per-node medians. (e) The thermal co-tenant floor (~77°C) observation is genuinely interesting and buried in one sentence — it deserves either a small figure or removal.

## Cross-cutting issues

**Anonymization is the most urgent problem.** Paper 1 is double-blind, but it names `%%burst` (a unique, searchable identifier of the public `nats-bursting` PyPI package), NRP Nautilus, the GV100 testbed, and the A10 verification run — all of which appear, attributed, in Paper 2 and its Zenodo/GitHub artifacts. A reviewer who Googles `%%burst` NATS deanonymizes Paper 1 in one query. Check INFOCOM's policy on pre-existing public artifacts; many venues accept best-effort anonymity, but you should at minimum rename or genericize the magic in Paper 1's text and scrub the `ssu-atlas-ai`-adjacent specifics. Conversely, verify SC26's anonymization requirements for your track — the SC papers program has used double-anonymous review in recent years, and Paper 2 currently carries your name, ORCID, GitHub org, and cluster namespace. If this version is a revision/camera-ready that's fine; if it's under review, it may be non-compliant.

**A factual tension between the papers.** Paper 1 verifies an A10 GPU worker running real CUDA matmul end-to-end on Nautilus (§VII), while Paper 2's threats section says "a GPU-image run [on the cluster] remains" unmeasured. "Verified" vs. "benchmarked" is a real distinction, but a reviewer holding both will ask — one clarifying clause in Paper 2 resolves it.

**A rhetorical tension worth pre-empting.** Paper 2's framing is "the politeness controller backs off rather than crowding tenants," while Paper 1's Fig. 5 shows AIMD harming the neighbor *more than naive* (45% vs. 38%) under memoryless contention. That's an honest and important finding, but it means "polite" is a regime-qualified property, and Paper 2's unqualified claims should be softened by one clause ("under structured or slack load") so the pair is self-consistent.

Overall: Paper 1 is a strong theory contribution once the Markov-vs-general scoping is fixed and, ideally, one live adaptive-controller run is added; Paper 2 is a genuinely good experience paper whose main gap is a direct demonstration of its titular mechanism. The anonymization interplay is the thing to fix this week, not at rebuttal time.
